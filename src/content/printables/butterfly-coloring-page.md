## A mirror game on the big butterfly

The left and right halves of the garden butterfly are exact mirror images. Every spot on one wing has a twin in the same place on the other wing. That makes a simple symmetry game for the kitchen table:

1. Your child colors only the left wings, any colors they like.
2. Hand the sheet back and ask them to copy the pattern on the right side, spot for spot.
3. Fold the paper along the body and hold it to a window. Do the colors line up?

Younger kids can play it the other way round. You color one spot on the left, and they find and color its twin. For older kids, try a "broken mirror" round: you change one spot on purpose, and they hunt for the mistake.

## What the life cycle wheel teaches

The <a href="https://www.fs.usda.gov/wildflowers/pollinators/Monarch_Butterfly/biology" target="_blank" rel="noopener">USDA Forest Service monarch biology page</a> names the four stages as egg, larva (caterpillar), pupa (chrysalis) and adult, and calls this complete metamorphosis. The wheel uses the same names, with the everyday word first and the science word in brackets, so kids can learn both.

| Stage | On the sheet | A fact to share while coloring |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Egg | Two eggs on a leaf | A monarch egg hatches about four days after it is laid, says the Forest Service. |
| 2. Caterpillar (larva) | A caterpillar on a leaf | Caterpillars need specific host plants, according to the National Park Service. Monarchs lay their eggs on milkweed. |
| 3. Chrysalis (pupa) | A chrysalis hanging from a twig | A monarch can go from chrysalis to adult in as few as five days. |
| 4. Butterfly (adult) | A small butterfly | Summer monarchs live about 2 to 5 weeks, but the generation that migrates can live up to 9 months. |

After coloring, cut the four circles apart, mix them up and ask your child to put them back in order. Then ask: "Which stage comes after the butterfly?" The arrow from 4 back to 1 is the answer, because adults lay new eggs.

## Butterfly body facts for curious kids

Kids often ask what the parts of the butterfly are. <a href="https://gardens.si.edu/learn/for-educators/cultivating-habitat/butterfly-gardening/about-butterflies" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Smithsonian Gardens</a> describes three main body segments, and our drawing shows each one as its own shape to color:

- **Head**, the round part at the top. It carries large compound eyes, club-shaped antennae used for smelling and finding the way, and a coiled tube called a proboscis that the butterfly uncurls like a straw to sip nectar.
- **Thorax**, the short oval in the middle. The wings and legs attach here.
- **Abdomen**, the long striped part at the bottom.

Look at the little round tips on the antennae in our picture. Those are the "clubs" the Smithsonian describes. Smithsonian Gardens also explains that butterfly wings are covered in tiny scales that overlap like shingles on a roof. The spots on the sheet are a good place to talk about this: real wing patterns are made of thousands of colored scales.

One more fact for a sunny day: the <a href="https://www.nps.gov/romo/learn/nature/butterfly-ecology.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">National Park Service</a> notes that butterflies cannot make their own body heat, so they rest with their wings turned toward the sun to warm up. That is why you rarely see them flying on cloudy or rainy days.
