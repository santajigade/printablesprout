Christmas Day falls on Friday, December 25 this year, according to the <a href="https://www.wiwb.uscourts.gov/node/28" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2026 federal holiday schedule</a>, which leaves plenty of December afternoons for a quiet table activity. This color by number turns a cozy holiday room into a picture that appears one number at a time: a tall tree with ornaments and a star, a stocking, a wreath, a frosty window, three wrapped presents and a toy train. Five little bells are tucked into the scene, and finding them is the mission at the bottom of the sheet.

## One picture, three levels

Use the **Version** buttons above the sheet to choose the level before you print or start coloring online.

| Version | What kids see in each space | Good fit for |
|---|---|---|
| Count the dots | A dice pattern of 1 to 6 dots | Ages 3 to 5, before numerals feel easy |
| Numbers 1 to 6 | One plain number | Ages 4 to 7 |
| Addition sums | A small sum such as 2+2 or 4+1 | Ages 6 to 8, first grade math practice |

All three versions share the same six color key: 1 red, 2 green, 3 yellow, 4 blue, 5 brown and 6 orange. That means siblings can work on different levels and still end up with matching pictures to hang side by side.

## The bell hunt mission

The bottom of the sheet turns coloring into a search. Bells are hiding on the wall, in the tree and on the floor. When your child finds them all, they color the right answer from three number circles. A simple way to run it:

1. **Color first, hunt second.** Every bell is a 3, so coloring the yellow spaces makes the bells pop out.
2. **Count with a finger.** Ask your child to touch each bell once as they count, which keeps them from counting the same bell twice.
3. **Check the answer together.** If the count and the circle do not match, hunt again instead of telling them.

Counting real objects is exactly the kind of practice the CDC suggests for this age. Its <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/4-years.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">milestones page for 4 year olds</a> encourages parents to count simple things like fingers, toys or bananas, and to help children learn about colors, shapes and sizes. The same page lists naming a few colors and holding a crayon between the fingers and thumb as milestones by age 4, so the dots version fits right where many preschoolers are.

## How to color it on a tablet

The sheet works online with tap to fill. Choose a color from the palette, tap a space to fill it, and use Undo if a tap lands in the wrong spot. The color key circles at the bottom are fillable too, so kids can color the key first and keep it as a guide. When the picture is done, the Download button saves it as an image to send to grandparents.

For the printed version, use crayons or colored pencils rather than markers on the small spaces like the ornaments and bows. Thin paper and markers can bleed through and blur the numbers next door.

## Ways to stretch it for older kids

- **Speed round:** time how long it takes to find all five bells before any coloring starts.
- **Make your own sums:** older kids write a different sum for each color on the back, such as 10 minus 4 for orange, then swap with a sibling.
- **Story starter:** ask who lives in this room and what the toy train is carrying. Write one sentence under the picture.
