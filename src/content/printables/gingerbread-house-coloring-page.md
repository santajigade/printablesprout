A gingerbread house is the one house kids are allowed to cover in candy, and this coloring page gives them plenty to work with. The roof is lined with round candies under a star, icing drips along the edge, peppermints and candy canes frame the door, and gumdrops sit along the bottom of the walls. A gingerbread friend waves from the snow next to two lollipops. Type a name and the title reads "Noah's Gingerbread House".

## Two roofs to choose from

Pick a version above the sheet before you print or color online.

- **Candy roof:** 30 round candies in neat rows with a star at the top. Every candy is its own shape, so tap to fill colors them one at a time. This is the counting version.
- **Design your own roof:** the roof is left empty. Kids draw their own candies, shingles or icing patterns, then color the rest of the house. It is a good pick for children who like to plan a real gingerbread house before building one.

## The candy counting game

Counting the candies turns a coloring page into a quick math game. Read a line from the table, let your child find and count those items, then color them. The answer column is for grown ups.

| Find and count | Answer |
|---|---|
| Round candies on the roof (candy roof version) | 30 |
| Gumdrops along the bottom of the walls | 4 |
| Candy canes next to the door | 2 |
| Slices in one peppermint | 8 |
| Window panes on the whole house | 8 |
| Lollipops in the snow | 2 |
| Candy stepping stones on the path | 3 |

For older kids, add a pattern rule before they start: color the roof candies red, green, red, green along each row, or make every row a different color. Five year olds can try "color all the gumdrops the same color", which is a simpler rule to hold in mind.

## A gingerbread story to tell while they color

Gingerbread houses have a longer history than most kids expect. The Library of Congress explains that the cottage of cake and candy in the Grimms' fairy tale <a href="https://blogs.loc.gov/families/2025/12/gingerbread-a-treat-for-the-holidays/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"Hansel and Gretel" is often credited with inspiring decorated gingerbread houses</a>. Long before that, Queen Elizabeth I of England handed out gingerbread figures to favored guests at dinner, which is a fun fact to share while your child colors the gingerbread friend.

There is a gingerbread house at the White House too. According to the <a href="https://whitehousehistory.org/white-house-gingerbread-holiday-traditions-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener">White House Historical Association</a>, assistant executive chef Hans Raffert made a German style A-frame gingerbread house for the holidays in 1969, and it grew into a yearly tradition for White House pastry chefs. In 1993 the pastry team made a nearly 100 pound copy of the White House itself. Ask your child what they would put on a gingerbread house big enough for a president.

## Ideas by age

| Age | Try this |
|---|---|
| 4 to 5 | Color the big shapes first: the walls, the roof and the snow. Count the gumdrops together. |
| 6 to 7 | Do the full counting table, then color the peppermints in two colors that take turns. |
| 8 to 9 | Use the blank roof version and draw a roof pattern that repeats, then write a short recipe for the house on the back. |

## Printing and coloring tips

The page prints in black line art on US Letter or A4. The candies are small, so fine markers or colored pencils work better than chunky crayons for the roof. On a tablet, tap to fill handles the small shapes easily, and Undo fixes a candy colored by mistake. If you print several copies for a class, the blank roof version gives every child a different looking house for a December display.
