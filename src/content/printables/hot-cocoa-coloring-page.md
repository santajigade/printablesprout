A mug of cocoa after playing outside is one of the simplest winter treats. This page gives kids a big steaming mug with a heart and two stars on it, a rim of cocoa, marshmallows bobbing on top, a saucer, two chocolate chip cookies and a checked tablecloth to finish in any pattern they like. Type a name and the title changes to "Leo's Hot Cocoa".

## The marshmallow count

Before any crayons come out, play a quick counting round. There are **seven marshmallows** on the sheet: four in the mug and three that have spilled onto the saucer. Ask:

1. How many marshmallows are in the mug?
2. How many fell onto the saucer?
3. How many is that altogether?

That turns the page into a first addition problem (4 plus 3 makes 7). Kids who are ready for more can count the chocolate chips too. Each cookie has six, so the two cookies hold twelve.

## Where cocoa comes from, in five steps

Kids are often surprised that cocoa starts as a seed on a tree. Here is the journey, using facts from <a href="https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/chocolate_science_history_and_fun_facts_part_1" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Michigan State University Extension's guide to chocolate history</a>. Read one step while your child colors each part of the mug.

| Step | What happens | Color while you read |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cacao trees grow in warm rainforests, mostly near the equator | The mug's top stripe |
| 2 | The pods grow right on the tree trunk | The heart |
| 3 | Each pod holds about 40 beans inside a white pulp | The two stars |
| 4 | The beans are the seeds used to make chocolate | The bottom stripe |
| 5 | Long ago in Mesoamerica, the Maya and Aztecs grew cacao and even used the beans as money | The cocoa in the mug |

Most cacao today is grown in West Africa, according to the same guide, and for most of history chocolate was a bitter drink rather than a sweet one. Older kids might like the scientific name of the tree, *Theobroma cacao*, which means "food of the gods."

## Warm, not hot: a quick check for real cocoa time

If the coloring session comes with a real mug, a few habits help. These come from the American Academy of Pediatrics page on <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/english/health-issues/injuries-emergencies/pages/treating-and-preventing-burns.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">treating and preventing burns</a>, which lists hot liquids among the things to keep away from young children.

- **Keep hot drinks out of reach.** Set the grown-up mugs toward the middle of the table, not near the edge where a small hand can grab them. The AAP notes that even a small splash can scald a baby.
- **Never hold a baby and a hot drink at the same time.** Put one down first.
- **Stir microwaved cocoa well.** The AAP suggests stirring microwaved food or letting it stand for two minutes so the heat spreads evenly, since hot spots can hide in the middle.
- **Test before you serve.** Let the cocoa cool to warm, and add a splash of cold milk if it is still too hot to sip.

## Coloring ideas that look great

- **Make the tablecloth a pattern.** Alternate two colors for a classic checkerboard, or color every square in a row the same for stripes.
- **Leave the steam white.** It stands out best against a colored background.
- **Try a dark cocoa color** for the inside of the mug and pale pinks or blues for the marshmallows so they pop.
