"I'm bored" usually arrives on day two of winter break. This free checklist gives kids an answer they can find on their own: 20 screen-free ideas sorted into four boxes, **Move**, **Make**, **Calm** and **Together**, plus one blank "My idea" line in each box. Kids color a tick box each time they try something. At the bottom, 14 snowflakes make an active play tracker for a two week break. Type your child's name and their own four ideas before printing, and they appear right on the sheet.

## What is on the checklist

| Box | Printed ideas | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Move | Indoor obstacle course, the floor is lava, freeze dance party, animal walks, sock toss basketball | Burns energy on cold days when outdoor time is short |
| Make | Blanket fort, cardboard creation, paper airplane contest, comic strip, a silly song | Hands-on building and creating |
| Calm | A book, a coloring page, an audiobook, watching birds or clouds, a jigsaw puzzle | Quiet time after a busy morning |
| Together | Baking with a grown-up, a letter to family, a card or board game, a talent show, a story built one line at a time | Time with siblings, parents and relatives |

Every idea comes from two American Academy of Pediatrics articles on HealthyChildren.org: one on swapping screens for play during school breaks, and <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/managing-screen-use-during-long-cold-winters.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">a pediatrician's guide to managing screen use during long, cold winters</a>. We picked the ones that need few or no special supplies.

## Match the idea to what your child is really after

The AAP school break article makes a useful point: a screen usually fills a need, so look for a swap that fills the same need. Here is how the four boxes line up with what kids are often looking for:

| If your child wants... | Steer them to | Example from the sheet |
|---|---|---|
| To win at something | Move or Together | Sock toss basketball, a card game |
| To wind down | Calm | An audiobook, a jigsaw puzzle |
| To laugh | Together | A talent show, a story one line each |
| To make something cool | Make | A paper airplane contest |

The same article suggests asking kids to help with real jobs too, such as gathering ingredients or holding the grocery list, and warns that kids may push back when limits start. Its advice is to resist giving in and to be enthusiastic about the new activity.

## Setting up the sheet in five minutes

1. **Sit down together on the first day of break.** The AAP winter guide suggests making a family plan with clear tech-free times for weekdays, weekends and school breaks. Agree on when the list comes out, for example after lunch.
2. **Fill in the four "My idea" lines** with your child's own picks. Ideas they choose get tried first.
3. **Put the sheet where kids see it**, such as the fridge or the back of a bedroom door.
4. **Make a backup jar.** The same guide describes an "I'm bored" jar: each family member writes ideas on colored paper, using a different color for solo, group and parent and child ideas. Copy the ideas from the sheet into the jar for kids who like a surprise.
5. **Celebrate a full box.** The guide mentions small rewards like an extra taco night. A full row of colored tick boxes is a good reason for one.

## The snowflake tracker and daily movement

Each snowflake at the bottom stands for one day of break. Your child colors it on any day they played hard. According to <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/children.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">the CDC's physical activity overview for children</a>, kids ages 3 to 5 should be active throughout the day, for example jumping or riding a tricycle. Kids ages 6 to 17 need 60 minutes or more of moderate to vigorous activity each day, including vigorous activity on at least 3 days a week. The CDC lists climbing as muscle strengthening and jumping or running as bone strengthening, and kids need each of those at least 3 days a week too.

| Age | What counts as a colored snowflake |
|---|---|
| 4 to 5 | Lots of active play spread through the day, like animal walks and freeze dance |
| 6 to 8 | About an hour of active play, such as an obstacle course plus a dance party |
| 9 to 11 | An hour or more, with some vigorous play mixed in, like racing through an obstacle course |
