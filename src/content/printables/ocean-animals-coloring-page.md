This ocean animals coloring page puts a whole coral reef on one sheet: a smiling whale, a sea turtle with a patterned shell, an octopus waving all eight arms, two jellyfish, five fish, crabs and starfish on the sand, plus seaweed and three kinds of coral. Once the coloring is done, the bottom of the sheet becomes a game. Kids count the animals and color the circle with the right number. The **Explorer level** version swaps in harder questions, including four tiny shells hidden around the reef.

## Two ways to play the reef game

| Version | What kids answer | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Count the animals | How many fish, starfish, crabs and jellyfish | Ages 3 to 6 |
| Explorer level | Hidden shells, octopus arms, octopus hearts, turtle flippers | Ages 6 to 9 |

Both versions use the same picture, so a younger and an older child can each color their own copy and then quiz each other. The answer circles are fillable online too: tap a circle to color it in.

## Sea facts behind the explorer questions

The explorer level asks a few questions that the picture alone cannot answer. Here is where the answers come from, so you can share the reason, not just the number.

**Octopus hearts.** The <a href="https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/news/feb26/undersea-creatures-valentines-day.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NOAA National Ocean Service</a> says an octopus has three hearts: two pump blood to the gills and the third sends blood to the rest of the body. Smithsonian Magazine adds that copper in octopus blood makes it look blue, which is a great excuse to color the octopus any shade your child likes.

**Starfish surprise.** The same NOAA article says sea stars have no hearts at all, and some kinds can have 10, 20 or even 40 arms. Ask your child to count the arms on the starfish they colored, then imagine one with 40.

**Sea turtles.** <a href="https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/sea-turtles" target="_blank" rel="noopener">NOAA Fisheries</a> says six species of sea turtles are found in U.S. waters, and all of them are protected under the Endangered Species Act. Adult females come up on land to lay their eggs, and leatherback turtles can travel 10,000 miles or more each year.

**Coral reefs.** NOAA Fisheries notes that reefs occur in less than 1 percent of the ocean yet are home to nearly one quarter of all ocean species. The National Park Service says it has ten parks with coral reefs, from the Pacific Ocean to the Atlantic.

## A five minute reef game for two players

1. Each player colors their own sheet.
2. Player one secretly picks an animal on the reef and gives one clue, such as "I have eight arms."
3. Player two points to the animal. A correct guess earns a sticker or a tally mark.
4. Switch roles. After five rounds each, count the tally marks.

Younger kids can give color clues instead ("I am the orange fish"), which makes the game a color naming practice too.

## Coloring tips for small sea creatures

The fish stripes, turtle shell plates and shells are separate spaces, so kids can make each one a different color. On paper, colored pencils work best for the tiny shells and starfish. For a bright ocean, color the water last with the side of a light blue crayon so the animals stay easy to see.
