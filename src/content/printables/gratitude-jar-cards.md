A gratitude jar is simple: family members write down something they are thankful for, fold the paper and drop it in a jar, then read the notes together later. The hard part is the blank page. This sheet fixes that with eight cut-out cards, each with a small picture to color and a question that gives kids somewhere to start. Type your family name and the title becomes "Garcia's Gratitude Jar". Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26, which makes this week a natural time to start a jar.

## What is on the two versions

- **Question cards.** Eight prompts with pictures: who helped you today, what made you smile this week, what food you are thankful for, what you love about home, what you like to see outside, who you could thank with a note, and what new thing you can do now. The eighth card is yours to edit.
- **Blank cards.** The same eight pictures with "I am thankful for..." and two lines, for kids who already have something in mind.

Print a few copies of each so the jar never runs out.

## Set up the jar in 10 minutes

1. **Find a jar.** Any clean jar, box or tin works. Kids can tape a colored card to the front as a label.
2. **Color first, then cut.** Color the pictures while the cards are still on one sheet, then cut along the dashed lines. Scissors stay with a grown-up for younger kids.
3. **Put the question cards in a cup next to the jar.** Each day, someone pulls one out and answers it.
4. **Answer on the card.** Write or draw the answer, fold it and drop it in the jar.
5. **Pick a reading night.** Thanksgiving dinner is a good first one. Take turns pulling out notes and reading them aloud.

The AAP's pediatrician guide to <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/emotional-wellness/Building-Resilience/Pages/how-to-practice-gratitude.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">nurturing gratitude in children</a> suggests talking or writing about at least one thing that went right each day, for example at bedtime, and collecting those moments in a family gratitude jar. It also suggests keeping the habit going after Thanksgiving, on the drive home or at game night.

## Match the questions to your child's age

Gratitude grows in steps. A Greater Good Science Center article on <a href="https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/how_to_help_gratitude_grow_in_your_kids" target="_blank" rel="noopener">how gratitude develops in kids</a> explains that toddlers may learn to say "thank you", but children between preschool and kindergarten are still learning to see things from another person's point of view. That changes which cards work best.

| Age | Cards that work well | How to help |
|---|---|---|
| 4 and 5 | Food, home, outside, smile | Ask what it looked, tasted or felt like. Let them draw the answer. |
| 6 and 7 | Who helped you, new thing you can do | Ask "How do you think that person felt?" |
| 8 to 10 | Who could you thank with a note, your own question | Turn one answer into a real thank you note that week. |

The APA makes a similar point: for toddlers and preschoolers, sharing the good feeling itself is enough, so "the cocoa was warm and yummy" is a great answer.

## Keep it fun instead of a chore

- **Grown-ups answer too.** The AAP and the APA both stress being a role model. When kids hear a parent say what they are thankful for, they copy it.
- **Do not force a daily answer.** The APA article points out that a gratitude habit does not have to be daily to help, and that some families respond better to looser, playful versions. Two or three cards a week is plenty.
- **Accept silly answers.** "My socks" still counts. Ask what is good about the socks.
- **Use the eighth card.** Change its question every week so the jar stays fresh.
