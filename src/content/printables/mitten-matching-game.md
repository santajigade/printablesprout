Every winter, mittens go missing one at a time. This sheet turns that everyday puzzle into a matching game. Six mittens on the left each have a partner on the right with the same pattern: stripes, dots, hearts, stars, a zigzag or a snowflake. Kids draw a line from each mitten to its match, then color the pairs to look alike. Type a name and the title becomes "Nora's Mitten Match".

## Three ways to play

Pick a version with the **Version** buttons above the sheet. The page opens with the drawing pen, so kids can draw the lines right on the screen.

- **Draw lines.** Numbers 1 to 6 sit on the left and letters A to F on the right. Kids draw from the black dot next to each mitten to the dot beside its match.
- **Memory cards.** Twelve mittens in dashed boxes. Color each pair, cut out the cards, turn them face down and take turns flipping two at a time.
- **Answer key.** Dashed lines show every correct pair, handy for a grown-up checking a stack of sheets.

## Why matching is real math for preschoolers

Matching is more than a time filler. The University of Nevada, Reno Extension describes matching as <a href="https://extension.unr.edu/4h/pub.aspx?PubID=2680" target="_blank" rel="noopener">a simple form of sorting</a>, and notes that children between 3 and 4 enjoy sorting objects by one trait such as color, shape or size. Later they learn to sort by two or more traits at once. The same guide says recognizing patterns supports math skills and helps children predict what comes next.

On this sheet the trait is the pattern on the mitten. All twelve mittens share the same outline, so a child has to look past the shape and focus on the details inside it, which is exactly the skill being practiced.

## Make it easier or harder

| Age | Start here | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Cover the bottom three rows with paper and match just three pairs | Name each pattern out loud: "dots", "stars" |
| 4 | Match all six pairs with lines | Color each pair the same two colors |
| 5 | Play the memory card game with six cards (three pairs) | Add the other six cards |
| 6 | Play memory with all twelve cards | Invent a seventh pattern and draw a new pair on the back |

If a child gets stuck, point to one small detail ("this one has hearts, can you find more hearts?") instead of pointing to the answer.

## The real-life matching round

When the sheet is done, take the game to the coat hooks. Mix a few real mittens, gloves and socks in a basket and ask your child to find the pairs. The Nevada extension guide suggests a similar sock-matching activity, and it gives kids a reason to keep track of their own mittens.

It is also a good moment to talk about why mittens matter. The CDC's <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/winter-weather/safety/stay-safe-during-after-a-winter-storm-safety.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">winter weather safety guidance</a> lists mittens, noting that they are warmer than gloves, along with a hat and layers of clothing. The same page points out that wet clothing chills the body quickly, so a soggy pair after snow play is a sign to swap in a dry one.

## Tips for the memory card version

- **Print on card stock** if you have it, so the colors do not show through when the cards are face down.
- **Color before cutting.** Small cards are hard to hold still while coloring.
- **Lay them in a grid** of three rows and four columns so it is easier to remember where each mitten was.
- **Keep scissors with a grown-up.** Cutting along the dashed lines is great practice for 5 and 6 year olds with child-safe scissors and supervision.
