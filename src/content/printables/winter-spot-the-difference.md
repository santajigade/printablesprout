A snowy yard sits at the top of this sheet: a little house with a smoking chimney, a pine tree with a star, a snowman in a top hat, a bird on the fence and a sled waiting by the tree. The picture underneath looks the same at first glance, but things have gone missing. Kids find each change, circle it, and color one "I found" circle for every difference they spot. Type a name and the title becomes "Ava's Spot the Difference".

## Pick the right version

Use the **Version** buttons above the sheet. The page opens with the drawing pen, so kids can circle the changes right on the screen and print the sheet afterward.

| Version | What changes | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| 5 differences | Only big objects disappear: hat, chimney, star, bird, sled | Ages 4 and 5, or a first try |
| 8 differences | Adds three small snowman details: a button, the scarf end, one arm | Ages 6 to 8, or a second round |
| Answer key | All 8 changes circled with dashed lines | Grown-ups checking work |

The top picture is the same in every version, so a child who finishes the 5 differences sheet can switch to 8 and keep going on the same scene.

## A search plan for little detectives

Spot the difference is easier with a method than with random looking. Teach this four step plan and say it out loud together the first time.

1. **Top to bottom.** Look at the sky first, then the roof and treetop, then the snowman, then the ground.
2. **One object at a time.** Point to the house in the top picture, then the house in the bottom picture. Count its parts: walls, roof, window, door, chimney. Anything missing?
3. **Count small things.** Buttons, arms and sticks are easy to miss. Count them in both pictures.
4. **Color a circle.** Each time you find one, color an "I found" circle. When every circle is colored, you are done.

When a child gets stuck, give a hint about where to look ("check the snowman's tummy") rather than pointing at the answer. The AAP's guide to <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/3-ways-your-child-builds-important-life-skills-through-play.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">building life skills through play</a> calls this kind of help scaffolding, and it suggests table-top activities such as puzzles to help children practice concentration.

## What your child practices

Looking for tiny changes calls for careful, patient looking, much like a hunt or a game of hide and seek. The AAP's <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/power-of-play/Pages/play-tips-for-preschool-age-children.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">play tips for preschoolers</a> list scavenger hunts and hide and seek as activities that build observation and attention skills. The same AAP guide notes that between ages 5 and 7, children start to develop attentional control and planning, and enjoy projects that need patience and focus on detail.

Praise the searching, not just the score. AAP advice is to focus on a child's effort rather than on how well the result meets a standard, so "you checked every button, that was careful looking" works better than "you got them all".

## Turn it into a two player game

- **Race with a timer.** Two children each get a printed copy. Set a timer for three minutes and see who finds more. Then swap sheets and check each other with the answer key.
- **Take turns.** On one sheet, players take turns naming a difference. If you name one that is already circled, you lose your turn.
- **Make your own.** On the back, a child draws a simple winter picture twice and leaves out three things in the second one, then challenges a grown-up to find them.
