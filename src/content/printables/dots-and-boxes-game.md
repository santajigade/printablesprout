Dots and boxes needs nothing but a grid of dots and two players. Take turns drawing one line between two dots that sit next to each other. If your line closes the fourth side of a box, that box is yours, and you take another turn. When every box is closed, the player with the most boxes wins.

This free printable gives you three board sizes, so the game grows with your child:

| Version | Boards | Boxes per game | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four nine-box games | 4 small grids | 9 | Ages 5 to 7, first games, waiting rooms |
| Two 25-box games | 2 medium grids | 25 | Ages 7 to 10, a best of two match |
| One 64-box game | 1 big grid | 64 | Ages 10 and up, families in teams |

Each game has a score area with a circle for each player and a box to write the number of boxes won. Type the player names before you print and they appear on the sheet.

## Playing on a tablet with the pen and fill tools

The page opens with the drawing pen, so two kids can play right on the screen. Pick a color for each player and color the circle next to that player's name. Draw lines with the pen. When someone closes a box, switch to tap to fill and tap the inside of the box in that player's color. Every box on the board is a fillable shape, so the finished grid shows at a glance who won which boxes. Count the colors, write the totals in the score boxes and print the result if your kids want to keep it.

## House rules that keep it fair for younger kids

Small kids enjoy the game more when the rules are spelled out before the first line:

1. **One line per turn**, only between two dots next to each other, across or down. No diagonals.
2. **Close a box, claim it.** Write your initial inside or color it, then draw one more line.
3. **Two boxes with one line** still earns just one extra turn. Science News describes this rule for double boxes.
4. **No erasing.** A line stays once it is drawn.

For a five or six year old, start with the nine-box boards. A full game takes a few minutes, which fits a short attention span, and losing one game is easy when three more are waiting on the same sheet.

## Strategy tips by age

The game looks simple, but mathematicians have studied it for decades. Science News describes the work of Elwyn Berlekamp, a mathematician at the University of California, Berkeley, who wrote that he learned the game in 1946 and later wrote a whole book about it, *The Dots-and-Boxes Game: Sophisticated Child's Play*. Here is how to share the ideas at different ages.

| Age | One idea to teach | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| 5 to 6 | Do not draw the third side | "If you draw the third side of a box, the next player gets it." |
| 7 to 8 | Count safe moves | "Before you draw, check if your line gives away a box." |
| 9 to 10 | Chains | "A row of boxes that are all ready to close is a chain. Whoever opens it gives it away." |
| 11 and up | Keep control | "Take all of a long chain except the last two boxes, and leave those two for your opponent." |

The last tip comes from <a href="https://www.math.ucla.edu/~tom/Games/dots&boxes_hints.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UCLA mathematician Thomas Ferguson's strategy notes</a>, which call a chain "long" when it has at least three boxes. Giving away two boxes on purpose forces the other player to open the next chain, and the player who stays in control often wins the most boxes. <a href="https://www.sciencenews.org/article/dots-and-boxes" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Science News</a> adds a surprise for older kids: in the nine-box game, an alert player who goes second can always win. Ask your child to play ten nine-box games, half going first and half going second, and see if the results agree.

## Why a simple dot game is worth printing

Dots and boxes asks kids to plan a move ahead, notice patterns and count, all without a single worksheet problem. It also teaches losing gracefully, since even strong players give away a few boxes every game. Keep a few printed sheets in the car or a restaurant bag, along with a pencil.
