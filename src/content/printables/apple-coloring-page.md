This apple coloring page is a whole orchard on one sheet. There is a big apple tree with nine apples, one of them with a smiling worm poking out, a ladder leaning on the trunk, a basket piled with apples, a tub of water where three apples float, a picket fence, buzzing bees and a sunny sky. In the corner sits a surprise: an apple cut across the middle, with a star of seeds inside. When the coloring is done, the box at the bottom turns the picture into a counting game.

## Two games on one orchard

| Version | Questions | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Count the apples | Apples on the tree, in the basket, in the tub, and all of them together | Ages 3 to 6 |
| Orchard explorer | Bees to find, points on the seed star, and two apple fact questions | Ages 5 to 8 |

Kids answer by coloring the circle with the right number, on paper or with a tap on the online version. The "all the apples" question asks children to add three groups, so it works as a stretch goal for kindergarten. Answers are in the FAQ below.

## Apple facts behind the explorer questions

The explorer level asks two yes or no questions that need a fact, not just counting. Here is where the answers come from.

**Apples float.** The <a href="https://www.nj.gov/agriculture/farmtoschool/documents/seasonality-chart/F2S%20Apples.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">New Jersey Department of Agriculture apple chart</a> says apples float because a quarter of their volume is air. That is why the apples in the tub are bobbing at the top.

**Apples are in the rose family.** The same chart says apples belong to the rose family and grow in every state in the U.S. It also says there are more than 7,000 apple varieties grown around the world. The <a href="https://extension.msstate.edu/publications/happyhealthy-fact-sheet-apples" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Mississippi State University Extension apple fact sheet</a> says over 2,500 varieties are grown each year, so there is a lot of choice at the store.

**The seed star.** Cut an apple across the middle, side to side, and you see a star. The New Jersey chart calls the seed pockets carpels. Count the points on the star you colored, then check it against a real apple.

## Turn the sheet into an apple tasting

This is the "keep going" part for a second sitting. Pick up two or three kinds of apples, such as the Gala, Fuji and Granny Smith named on the Mississippi State fact sheet.

1. Wash the apples together. Mississippi State Extension lists washing apples as a job kids can help with.
2. A grown up cuts one apple across the middle so everyone can see the star.
3. Taste a slice of each kind. Leave the skin on: Utah State University Extension says the skin holds most of the nutrition.
4. Your child colors the apples on the sheet to match the favorite one: red, yellow or green.

If you have extra slices, Mississippi State suggests dipping them in water with a little lemon juice so they do not turn brown as fast.

## Coloring tips for a bright orchard

Each apple has its own leaf, so kids can make the leaves green and the apples any color they like. The worm, the bees and the ladder are small, so colored pencils help there. Color the sky and grass last with the side of a crayon so the apples stay easy to count.
