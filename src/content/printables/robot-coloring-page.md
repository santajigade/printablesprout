This robot coloring page is a little workshop adventure. A friendly helper robot has lost some parts: four gears and six bolts are scattered around the room, and your child's job is to find each one and color a square in the mission box. The robot itself has plenty to color, from the antenna ball and screen face to the heart light, three buttons, four battery bars, claw hands and tread wheels. Switch the sheet to the **Mars rover** version for a second mission on a rocky planet, with a flying drone, craters and eight rock samples to collect.

## The two missions on the sheet

| Version | Mission | Extra challenge |
|---|---|---|
| Helper robot repair | Find 4 gears and 6 bolts, color a box for each | Color the 4 battery bars to "charge" the robot |
| Mars rover rock hunt | Find 8 rocks for the rover to study | Answer the wheel question at the bottom |

Type your child's name and the robot's name before printing. The title changes to their name, and the robot's name prints on its own line, which makes the page feel like their own invention.

## What makes a robot a robot?

Coloring is a great moment for a short science chat. NASA's <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-robotics-grades-k-4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">robotics page for kids</a> describes robots simply as machines that can be used to do jobs. Some robots work by themselves, and others always need a person to tell them what to do. A few real examples to talk about while your child colors:

- **Robot arms in space.** The space station has a big robot arm called Canadarm2, with a smaller robotic hand named Dextre for small jobs. People steer them with controllers that work like joysticks.
- **A robot shaped like a person.** NASA built Robonaut with a head, upper body, arms and hands, and cameras in its head. It went to the space station in 2011 to help astronauts.
- **Robots that explore.** NASA Space Place points out that robots do not need to eat or sleep and can last in space for many years, which is why they go to places that are too far or too harsh for people.

Ask your child which job their helper robot does. The claw hands, the screen face and the antenna are all clues they can use in their answer.

## Real rover numbers to share

The rover version is a good bridge to a real machine. According to the <a href="https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/press_kits/mars_2020/launch/mission/spacecraft/perseverance_rover/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">JPL press kit for the Perseverance rover</a>:

| Rover fact | Perseverance |
|---|---|
| Size | About 10 feet long, 9 feet wide and 7 feet tall |
| Weight on Earth | 2,260 pounds |
| Wheels | 6 |
| Robotic arm | About 7 feet long, with five joints |
| Cameras | 19, plus two microphones |

Try this: measure 10 feet across a room or hallway with a tape measure so your child can see how long the rover really is.

## Make it last longer

- **Ages 4 to 5:** color the robot and hunt for the big gears first. Help with counting the bolts.
- **Ages 6 to 7:** do both versions on different days and compare which mission was harder.
- **Ages 8 and up:** on the back of the sheet, draw a new tool for one claw hand and write one sentence about the job it does.
